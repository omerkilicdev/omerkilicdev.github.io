Why a spin needs a rotary-balance test

Aircraft spin aerodynamics · interactive lesson in 21 steps · self-study in flight dynamics

If the usual aerodynamic derivatives predict how an aircraft responds to small disturbances, why does a spin need its own wind-tunnel test, in which the model is turned steadily about the flow axis? The steps below build the answer from body axes up. Each tab sets up a 3D scene you can orbit and play, and the column beside it gives that step’s definitions, equations, units check and a worked number.

One example aircraft throughout: span b = 10 m wing area S = 40 m² speed V = 60 m/s air density ρ = 1.225 kg/m³

Step 0

The aircraft, its body axes and three rotation rates

The whole lesson uses one aircraft: a procedural model of a high-agility experimental jet, not any real type. Orbit it with the mouse; the parts are labelled.

  • Fuselage: at high angle of attack it makes lift of its own, and a wake that shields the tail.
  • Wing (cropped delta): the main lift source. The lesson turns on the left and right halves seeing different air.
  • Canards: small foreplanes for pitch control and for shaping the flow over the wing at high α.
  • Elevons: trailing-edge surfaces; deflected together they act as elevators, opposite as ailerons.
  • Fins and rudders: they control and damp yaw, the main actors of steps 3–5.
  • Intakes and nozzles: propulsion is left out of the lesson entirely.
  • Centre of gravity (CG): every rotation is about this point and every moment is taken about it.

Body axes

\(x\)longitudinal, positive towards the nose (ochre arrow)—
\(y\)lateral, positive towards the right wing (green)—
\(z\)vertical, positive down (red)—

Why z points down. Not because of gravity: x forward and y to the right make a right-handed set only with z down. The cross product \(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) used later takes its signs from this convention. The 3D library draws Y up on screen; the lesson always speaks in aircraft axes.

Rotation rates

\(p\)roll rate about x; positive = right wing downrad/s
\(q\)pitch rate about y; positive = nose uprad/s
\(r\)yaw rate about z; positive = nose rightrad/s
\(\vec\omega\)\(=(p,\,q,\,r)\), the single angular-velocity vectorrad/s

Units check: why the radian is dimensionless

arc length \(=\theta R\)[m] = [θ][m] ⇒ [θ] = 1
one turn \(=2\pi\) rad360° = 6.283 rad
\(r=\Delta\psi/\Delta t\)[rad]/[s] = [1/s]

Worked number

An aircraft completes one turn of a spin in 2 s: \(r\approx 2\pi/2\ \text{s}=3.14\) rad/s. Compared with \(V=60\) m/s later on, this turns out to be large.

In a spin, \(p\) and \(r\) matter most: a spin is roll and yaw together and sustained. \(q\) is present but secondary.

Step 1

Level flight: relative wind and angle of attack

Side view. The horizontal arrow is the air relative to the aircraft, the dashed ochre line is the wing chord, and the angle between them is α. Raise α past about 15° and the flow leaves the upper surface.

Definitions

Freestream velocity \(V_\infty\): the speed of the aircraft through undisturbed air. Relative wind: the same vector reversed, the air as seen from the cockpit. Chord line: the straight line from leading edge to trailing edge. Angle of attack \(\alpha\): the angle between chord line and relative wind.

Lift comes from turning the air downward, and how much it is turned depends on α. At small angles the relation is nearly linear; thin-aerofoil theory gives

\[ C_L \approx 2\pi\alpha \quad(\alpha \text{ in radians}). \]

α is the lift “throttle”, until it stops working (step 7).

Not the pitch attitude. The nose angle to the horizon is \(\theta\). α is measured to the air: an aircraft can have \(\theta=0\) and a very large α. In a spin the nose can point down while \(\alpha\approx45\)–\(90^\circ\), because the air comes from below.

Units check: degrees and radians

\(\alpha=5^\circ=5\cdot\pi/180\)= 0.0873 rad
\(\sin 0.0873=0.0872,\ \tan 0.0873=0.0875\)small angle: sin α ≈ tan α ≈ α
formulas always take radiansdegrees only for talking

Worked number

Cruise at \(V=60\) m/s and \(\alpha=5^\circ\): \(C_L\approx2\pi\cdot0.0873=0.55\). Attached flow, symmetric wings: the reference state for every comparison that follows.

Step 2

Do both wings see the same air?

The aircraft flies straight at α = 5°: no roll, no yaw, no pitch rate. Each wing tip carries a wind arrow and an α arc.

Question 1: do the left and right wings see the same speed and the same angle? Think, then open.

Yes. With \(p=q=r=0\) every point of a rigid body moves with the same velocity \(\vec V\), so every point sees the same relative wind. Add the left–right symmetry of the aircraft (the green plane) and the two wings have equal speed, equal angle and equal lift.

The rest of the lesson is about this symmetry breaking: why, and by how much, the two wings come to see different air. Here the difference is zero.

One more assumption: sideslip

Sideslip angle \(\beta\): the angle between the relative wind and the plane of symmetry. Here \(\beta=0\); it returns in the sum of step 6.

Worked number

\(V=60\) m/s, \(\alpha=5^\circ\): left tip 60 m/s and 5°, right tip 60 m/s and 5°. Difference 0 m/s and 0°. Both zeros stop being zero in steps 9 and 11.

Step 3

Rudder input: the yawing moment and its coefficient \(C_n\)

The rudder is deflected by δr. The tail makes a side force (ochre) and a turning tendency about the CG (red arc).

Definitions

Moment: the turning effect of a force about a point, force times perpendicular distance (the arm). Yawing moment \(N\): the moment about the z axis through the CG; positive turns the nose right (right-hand rule, z down).

Units check: rudder moment

\(N=-F_y\,l_t\)[N][m] = [N·m]
force to the right (+y), arm behind (−x)N < 0: nose left

Why a coefficient

\(N\) depends on size, speed and air density. To compare a 1/10-scale tunnel model with the aircraft, divide those out:

\[ C_n=\frac{N}{\bar q\,S\,b},\qquad \bar q=\tfrac12\rho V^2 \]
\(C_n\)yawing-moment coefficient; subscripts l, m, n are roll, pitch, yaw about x, y, z—
\(\bar q\)dynamic pressure, kinetic energy of the air per unit volumePa
\(\rho\)air densitykg/m³
\(S\)wing reference aream²
\(b\)wingspan, the length scale for lateral momentsm
\(\delta_r\)rudder deflectiondeg

Units check: is \(C_n\) dimensionless?

\(\bar q=\tfrac12\rho V^2\)[kg/m³][m²/s²] = [N/m²]
\(\bar q\,S\,b\)[N/m²][m²][m] = [N·m]
\(C_n=N/(\bar q S b)\)[N·m]/[N·m] = 1

Forces scale with \(\bar q S\) and arms with a length. For roll and yaw the arms run along the span, so the length is \(b\); pitch uses the mean chord \(\bar c\). It is a convention; what matters is that everyone uses the same one.

Worked number

\(\bar q=0.5\cdot1.225\cdot3600=2205\) Pa and \(\bar q S b=2205\cdot40\cdot10=882\,000\) N·m. A rudder giving \(C_n=-0.02\) produces \(N=-0.02\cdot882\,000=-17\,640\) N·m, about 1.8 tonne-metres turning the nose left.

Note the case: \(C_n\) is the yawing moment; \(C_N\) is the normal force coefficient. Papers use both side by side.

Step 4

Once the aircraft yaws: \(\hat r\) and \(C_{n_r}\)

First turn the yaw rate into a number comparable with \(V\), then derive the moment the rotation produces against itself. The scene has two modes.

1. Velocity of a point on a rotating body

A point at distance \(R\) from the CG moves on a circle; the path length is \(s=\theta R\), so

\[ v=\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{d\theta}{dt}R=rR. \]

The rotational speed grows with distance and is largest at the wing tip, \(R=b/2\).

Units check

\(v=rR\)[rad/s][m] = [m/s], comparable with V

2. Two geometries

The rotational velocity is always perpendicular to the radius. At a wing tip (A) the radius points sideways, so \(r\,b/2\) lies along \(V\) and simply adds: a ratio. At a point on the body axis b/2 ahead of the CG (B) the radius points forward, so \(r\,b/2\) is perpendicular to \(V\): a right triangle and a tangent.

3. Definition of \(\hat r\)

\[ \hat r=\frac{r\,b/2}{V}=\frac{rb}{2V}=\tan\varphi \]

Units check

\(\hat r=(r\,b/2)/V\)[m/s]/[m/s] = 1
\(\tan\varphi=\) opposite/adjacentthe same dimensionless number
small angle \(\tan\varphi\approx\varphi\)r̂ = 0.25 ≈ 14°

Worked numbers

Normal flight: \(r=0.5\) rad/s, \(b/2=5\) m, \(r\,b/2=2.5\) m/s, \(\hat r=2.5/60=0.042\). Tips (A): \(60\pm2.5\) m/s. Point B: \(\varphi=2.4^\circ\).

Spin: \(r=3\) rad/s, \(r\,b/2=15\) m/s, \(\hat r=0.25\). Tips: 75 and 45 m/s. Point B: \(\varphi=14^\circ\).

\(\hat r\) is a ratio of two speeds, not a speed. The right triangle appears only where the rotational velocity is normal to \(V\); at the tips the two add along one line. (“Helix angle” properly belongs to roll, \(\hat p=pb/2V\), where the tip velocity is normal to \(V\).)

4. \(C_{n_r}\), a partial derivative

\[ C_{n_r}=\frac{\partial C_n}{\partial\hat r} \]

How much \(C_n\) changes when \(\hat r\) changes slightly with everything else held fixed: a slope. It is called a dynamic derivative, or the yaw-damping derivative.

5. From first principles: why the tail resists

(a) The fin is a wing turned 90°. A wing gives \(C_L=C_{L\alpha}\alpha\) at small angles (thin aerofoil \(2\pi\)/rad; real wings 4–6/rad; short, broad fins 2–3/rad). Swap the roles α → \(\beta_t\), \(L\) → \(F_y\), \(S\) → \(S_v\), \(C_{L\alpha}\) → \(C_{L\alpha,v}\):

\[ L=\bar q\,S\,C_{L\alpha}\,\alpha\ \longrightarrow\ F_y=\bar q\,S_v\,C_{L\alpha,v}\,\beta_t \]

(b) The angle the fin sees. The fin sits \(l_t\) behind the CG, on the body axis (geometry B). Yawing right, it sweeps left at \(r\,l_t\), so the air reaches it from the left:

\[ \tan\beta_t=\frac{r\,l_t}{V}\ \Rightarrow\ \beta_t\approx\frac{r\,l_t}{V}. \]

(c) Direction and arm. Air from the left pushes the fin right (+y), \(l_t\) behind the CG, so the nose turns left: \(N=-F_y\,l_t\). The rotation makes its own brake, whatever started it.

(d) Algebra. Substituting (a) and (b) into (c), writing \(r/V=2\hat r/b\) and dividing by \(\bar q S b\):

\[ N=-\bar q\,S_v\,C_{L\alpha,v}\,l_t^2\,\frac{r}{V}=-\bar q\,S_v\,C_{L\alpha,v}\,l_t^2\,\frac{2\hat r}{b} \] \[ C_n=-2\,C_{L\alpha,v}\,\frac{S_v}{S}\left(\frac{l_t}{b}\right)^2\hat r,\qquad C_{n_r}=\frac{\partial C_n}{\partial\hat r}=-2\,C_{L\alpha,v}\,\frac{S_v}{S}\left(\frac{l_t}{b}\right)^2 \]

Three factors, three reasons: how sensitive the fin is to angle, how large it is relative to the wing, and the tail arm squared (one \(l_t\) sets the sweep speed, the other the moment arm; double the arm, four times the damping). The minus sign: the brake opposes the rotation.

Units check along the chain

\(\beta_t=r\,l_t/V\)[1/s][m]/[m/s] = 1 (rad)
\(F_y=\bar q S_v C_{L\alpha,v}\beta_t\)[N/m²][m²][1/rad][rad] = [N]
\(N=-F_y l_t\)[N·m]
\(C_{n_r}\)[1/rad], in practice dimensionless
\(l_t\)tail arm, CG to the fin’s aerodynamic centrem
\(S_v\)fin aream²
\(C_{L\alpha,v}\)fin lift-curve slope (side-force slope in \(\beta_t\))1/rad
\(\beta_t\)local sideslip angle at the finrad
\(F_y\)fin side force, +y to the rightN

Worked number: the same chain in numbers

\(\bar q=2205\) Pa, \(b=10\) m, \(S=40\) m²; fin \(l_t=4.5\) m, \(S_v=4.8\) m², \(C_{L\alpha,v}=2.5\)/rad; normal flight \(r=0.5\) rad/s.

\(r\,l_t=2.25\) m/s, \(\beta_t=2.25/60=0.0375\) rad \(=2.1^\circ\). \(C_Y=2.5\cdot0.0375=0.094\), \(F_y=2205\cdot4.8\cdot0.094=995\) N to the right. \(N=-995\cdot4.5=-4\,480\) N·m. \(C_n=-4\,480/882\,000=-0.0051\).

Check with the formula: \(\hat r=0.5\cdot10/120=0.0417\); \(C_{n_r}=-2\cdot2.5\cdot0.12\cdot0.2025=-0.12\); \(C_n=-0.12\cdot0.0417=-0.0050\). Both routes agree.

The derivative is taken with respect to \(\hat r\), not \(r\), so it carries no seconds and transfers between model and aircraft. Behind every \(C_{n_r}\) in a book is \(rb/2V\).

Step 5

Damping: why the good news has a minus sign

The aircraft is given a yaw rate and released. Ochre arc: the rotation. Green arc: the aerodynamic moment. The rate falls with time.

Question 2: once the nose starts turning right, should the aerodynamic moment add to the rotation or oppose it?

Oppose it. A rotation that grew by itself would turn every gust into a spin. With \(r>0\) we want \(N<0\); since \(N=C_{n_r}\hat r\,\bar q S b\) and \(\hat r>0\), that needs \(C_{n_r}<0\). This is yaw damping, and \(C_{n_r}<0\) is normally good news.

Newton’s second law for rotation about z:

\[ I_z\frac{dr}{dt}=N=\bar q S b\,C_{n_r}\frac{rb}{2V} \]

The right side is proportional to \(r\), so \(dr/dt=-r/\tau\) with

\[ \tau=\frac{2VI_z}{\bar q\,S\,b^2\,|C_{n_r}|},\qquad r(t)=r_0\,e^{-t/\tau}. \]

\(\tau\) is the time constant, the time to lose 63% of the rate. The animation plays exactly this equation.

Units check: is \(\tau\) in seconds?

numerator \(2VI_z\)[m/s][kg·m²] = [kg·m³/s]
denominator \(\bar q S b^2|C_{n_r}|\)[kg/(m·s²)][m²][m²] = [kg·m³/s²]
ratio[s]
\(I_z\)mass moment of inertia about zkg·m²
\(\tau\)time constants
\(r_0\)initial yaw raterad/s

Worked number

\(I_z=60\,000\) kg·m², \(|C_{n_r}|=0.12\): \(\tau=\dfrac{2\cdot60\cdot60\,000}{2205\cdot40\cdot100\cdot0.12}=\dfrac{7.2\times10^6}{1.06\times10^6}\approx6.8\) s. A gust falls to a third in about 7 s and is nearly gone in 20 s.

The sign means direction relative to the motion: negative is a brake, positive is a throttle. Step 16 shows this sign reversing: \(C_{n_r,\text{eff}}>0\) is the engine of the spin.

Step 6

The classical approach: add the contributions

Three arcs: sideslip (ochre), yaw rate (green, a brake), rudder (red). The dark arc is their sum. Classical aerodynamics measures each effect separately and adds.

\[ C_n=C_{n_0}+C_{n_\beta}\beta+C_{n_r}\frac{rb}{2V}+C_{n_p}\frac{pb}{2V}+C_{n_{\delta_r}}\delta_r+\cdots \]
\(C_{n_0}\)moment with everything at zero (≈ 0 for a symmetric aircraft)—
\(C_{n_\beta}\)weathercock stability; positive wanted1/rad
\(C_{n_r}\)yaw damping (steps 4–5); negative wanted1/rad
\(C_{n_p}\)cross-coupling from roll rate into yaw1/rad
\(C_{n_{\delta_r}}\)rudder effectiveness1/rad
\(\hat p=pb/2V\)nondimensional roll rate—

Where it comes from: a Taylor expansion

\(C_n=f(\alpha,\beta,\hat p,\hat r,\delta_r,\dots)\) is unknown, but near a reference point a smooth function is close to its tangent plane:

\[ f(x_1,x_2,\dots)\approx f(0)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}x_1+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}x_2+\cdots \]

Each partial derivative is an aerodynamic derivative, and the formula is the first-order expansion: linear superposition. It works in normal flight because (1) the disturbances are small (β a few degrees, \(\hat r<0.05\)) and (2) the flow is attached, so the effects do not alter one another: 1 + 1 = 2.

Units check: every term dimensionless

\(C_{n_\beta}\beta\)[1/rad][rad] = 1 (β in radians)
\(C_{n_r}\hat r\)[1/rad]·1 = 1
\(C_{n_{\delta_r}}\delta_r\)degrees here would be a factor 57 error

Worked number

\(C_{n_\beta}=0.10\), \(\beta=8^\circ=0.14\) rad; \(C_{n_r}=-0.12\), \(\hat r=0.15\); \(C_{n_{\delta_r}}=-0.08\), \(\delta_r=10^\circ=0.175\) rad:

\[ C_n=0.10\cdot0.14+(-0.12)(0.15)+(-0.08)(0.175)=0.014-0.018-0.014=-0.018 \]

The net moment turns the nose left. The arcs in the scene follow this sum as you move the sliders.

Watch this

Here \(C_{n_r}\) is a constant: the moment grows in proportion to the rotation however fast it turns. Step 15 tests that assumption.

Step 7

Raise α to 40°: the flow separates

The streaks no longer follow the upper surface; a reversed, turbulent region covers the wing and the tail. The wing turns red with separation.

Question 3: can the flow over the upper surface still follow the wing?

Mostly not. Over the upper surface the air first speeds up (pressure falls) and must then slow towards the trailing edge (pressure rises). The thin boundary layer has lost energy to friction and cannot climb the rising pressure, so it leaves the surface: flow separation. As α grows the “hill” steepens and separation moves forward; at 40° almost the whole upper surface is separated.

  • Separation: a wide, low-pressure but disorderly region over the wing.
  • Vortex: on a delta wing the flow leaving the leading edge rolls into a tight vortex that adds lift at moderate α.
  • Vortex breakdown: at high α the vortex suddenly swells and bursts and the lift drops. Left and right need not burst together.
  • Wake and blanketing: the separated region is carried aft and buries the fin in low-energy air; the tail that made the damping in step 4 loses its effect.

The lift curve rises linearly, peaks and falls; the peak is the stall. For the example aircraft the peak is near 19° with \(C_L\approx1.42\), and \(C_L\approx0.9\) at 40° (chart in step 19). Past the peak \(dC_L/d\alpha<0\): more angle, less lift.

Why the linear model breaks. Of step 6’s two conditions, attached flow is gone. Effects now change one another: sideslip bursts one wing’s vortex, that wing’s lift drops, the wake reaching the tail changes. \(C_n\) is no longer a sum of separate effects but a nonlinear function to be measured as a whole.

Worked number

\(C_L(19^\circ)=1.42\), \(C_L(40^\circ)=0.90\): at twice the angle the wing makes 63% of the lift. The loss measures the separated region.

Step 8

Keep it turning: steady \(r>0\)

The aircraft keeps turning the same way. Red arrow: the angular velocity ω, along the body z axis (down). Watch the turn counter.

Definitions

Steady rotation: the angular velocity is constant in direction and size. In an oscillation the rate changes sign and averages to zero; here it never does. Here \(\vec\omega=(0,0,r)\).

Units check: the period of a turn

\(\hat r=0.25\Rightarrow r=\hat r\cdot2V/b=0.25\cdot120/10\)= 3.0 rad/s
\(T=2\pi/r=6.28/3.0\)= 2.1 s per turn

Worked number

One turn in 2.1 s is 29 turns a minute. Real spins have \(\hat r\) between 0.1 and 0.4, and light aircraft take 1–3 s per turn. Against the “normal” \(\hat r=0.04\) of step 4 that is 6 to 10 times larger.

The basic equation of rigid-body kinematics, for a point at \(\vec r_{\text{pt}}\) from the CG:

\[ \vec V_{\text{pt}}=\vec V_{\text{CG}}+\vec\omega\times\vec r_{\text{pt}} \]

\(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) is normal to both vectors, of size \(|\omega||r|\sin\theta\), by the right-hand rule. It is not zero at the tips and has opposite signs at the two tips, so step 2’s argument (“every point moves with the same \(\vec V\)”) no longer holds. The formula of step 6 includes \(\hat r\), but only linearly: ten times \(\hat r\), ten times the moment. Whether the flow at such a large \(\hat r\) is still the same flow is the real question; steps 9 and 11 say no.

Step 9

The two wings no longer see the same air

Top view. Eight wind arrows along the span: long on the left wing (green), short on the right (red), proportional to \(V_\infty\pm r\,y\) with no exaggeration.

With \(\vec\omega=(0,0,r)\) and a point on the wing \(\vec r_{\text{pt}}=(0,y,0)\), the cross product

\[ \vec a\times\vec b=(a_yb_z-a_zb_y,\ a_zb_x-a_xb_z,\ a_xb_y-a_yb_x) \]

gives

\[ \vec\omega\times\vec r_{\text{pt}}=(0\cdot0-r\,y,\ r\cdot0-0\cdot0,\ 0\cdot y-0\cdot0)=(-r\,y,\ 0,\ 0). \]

So \(\vec V_{\text{pt}}=(V-r\,y,\,0,\,0)\): the right wing (\(y>0\)) sees \(V-r\,y\), slower, on the inside of the turn; the left wing (\(y=-|y|\)) sees \(V+r|y|\), faster, sweeping forward on the outside.

\[ V_{\text{left}}\neq V_{\text{right}} \]

Units check

\(r\,y\)[rad/s][m] = [m/s]
\(V-r\,y\)[m/s] − [m/s] = [m/s]
\(r\,y/V=\hat r\cdot y/(b/2)\)dimensionless; exactly r̂ at the tip

The signs depend on the axis choice, the physics does not: the outer wing runs in the outer lane.

Worked numbers

Normal flight: \(r=0.5\) rad/s, \(y=\pm5\) m, \(r\,y=2.5\) m/s. Left tip 62.5 m/s, right tip 57.5 m/s: 8% apart.

Spin: \(\hat r=0.25\Rightarrow r=3\) rad/s, \(r\,y=15\) m/s. Left tip 75 m/s, right tip 45 m/s: 50% apart, the right tip at 60% of the left.

Step 10

Lift goes with the square of the speed

Two lift arrows, scaled with \((V_{\text{local}}/V_\infty)^2\): long on the left, short on the right. The red arc at the nose is the rolling moment the difference creates.

Question 4: if \(L=\tfrac12\rho V^2SC_L\) and the wings see different \(V\), can their lift be equal?

No, and the difference is larger than the speed difference. \(L\propto V^2\): an 8% speed difference gives \((1.08)^2=1.17\), 17% in lift; a 50% one gives \((75/45)^2=2.8\) times. Squaring magnifies small differences.

Bernoulli: \(p+\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is constant along the flow. \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is the kinetic energy per unit volume and the pressure recovered when the air is stopped: the dynamic pressure \(\bar q\). The pressure difference across the wing scales with \(\bar q\); times the area it is a force; shape and α go into \(C_L\):

\[ L=\bar q\,S\,C_L=\tfrac12\rho V^2S\,C_L \]

Units check

\(\tfrac12\rho V^2\)[kg/m³][m²/s²] = [N/m²]
\(\times S\)[N/m²][m²] = [N]
\(\times C_L\)dimensionless

Worked number

Spin case, \(\hat r=0.25\), mid-wing \(y=\pm3.5\) m (0.7 of the semispan): \(V_{\text{left}}=60(1+0.175)=70.5\), \(V_{\text{right}}=60(1-0.175)=49.5\) m/s. Ratio \((70.5/49.5)^2=2.03\): the left wing lifts twice as much as the right, even at the same α and \(C_L\).

More lift on the left rolls the aircraft right, \(p>0\). Yaw alone has produced roll: cross-coupling, represented by the derivative \(C_{l_r}\). A spin is never “just yaw”, and the lift difference acts with an arm of order \(b/2\).

Step 11

Worse in a spin: the local angles of attack differ

So far only the speed changed. Now the tip arrows come from different directions: a larger α at the right tip, a smaller one at the left. The colours show two different flow regimes.

In a real spin the aircraft also rolls, \(\vec\omega=(p,0,r)\), and the cross product gains a vertical part:

\[ \vec\omega\times(0,y,0)=(-r\,y,\ 0,\ p\,y) \]

The body components of the air velocity at the tip (forward \(u\), down \(w\)) are

\[ u=V\cos\alpha-r\,y,\qquad w=V\sin\alpha+p\,y,\qquad \alpha_{\text{local}}=\arctan\frac{w}{u}. \]

Rolling right, the right tip moves down, so it sees the air coming from below: a larger α. The rising left tip sees the opposite. Yaw changes \(u\): on the slowed right wing \(w/u\) grows and α rises again. On the right wing both effects push the same way.

Units check

\(V\cos\alpha-r\,y\)[m/s] − [rad/s][m] = [m/s]
\(V\sin\alpha+p\,y\)[m/s]
\(\arctan(w/u)\)ratio → angle (rad)

Worked number

\(\alpha=40^\circ\), \(\hat p=\hat r=0.10\), at the tip: \(u/V=\cos40^\circ-0.10=0.666\), \(w/V=\sin40^\circ+0.10=0.743\), so \(\alpha_{\text{right}}=\arctan(0.743/0.666)=48^\circ\). Left tip: \(u/V=0.866\), \(w/V=0.543\), \(\alpha_{\text{left}}=32^\circ\). A 16° difference, both beyond the 19° stall but to different depths.

  • Left wing (32°): the vortex may still be partly attached; moderate lift.
  • Right wing (48°): vortex burst, upper surface fully separated; low lift, high drag.

The key distinction

The aircraft is no longer “at 40° with a small yaw correction”. It is in a rotating separated-flow state: one wing in one regime, the other in another, their wakes mixing at the tail. That state cannot be built from data on a non-rotating aircraft; it can only be measured. Past the stall \(C_L(\alpha)\) is not linear, so the mean of \(C_L(32^\circ)\) and \(C_L(48^\circ)\) is not \(C_L(40^\circ)\): the average angle does not give the average force.

Step 12

The two tests of the classical model: static and forced oscillation

Two tunnel models: one fixed (test A), one rocked left and right at small amplitude (test B). The classical method adds their results to predict the rotating aircraft.

Test A: static

The model is held on a rear sting; α and β are stepped; an internal six-component balance reads \(C_L, C_D, C_m, C_l, C_n, C_Y\) at each point. The result is a table \(C_n(\alpha,\beta)\) with \(\hat p=\hat r=0\), from which static derivatives such as \(C_{n_\beta}\) follow.

Test B: forced oscillation

The model is driven sinusoidally about one axis, \(\psi(t)=\psi_0\sin\omega t\), at small amplitude (±2–8°). The measured moment splits into the part in phase with the motion (position-dependent, a static derivative) and the part 90° out of phase (rate-dependent, a damping derivative).

The yaw rate \(r=\dot\psi=\psi_0\,\omega\cos\omega t\) changes sign continuously and averages to zero. What comes out is the combination

\[ C_{n_r}-C_{n_{\dot\beta}}\cos\alpha, \]

because the oscillating model sees both \(r\) and \(\dot\beta\) and the two cannot be separated. \(C_{n_{\dot\beta}}\) comes from the lag of the flow (step 17).

Units check: how large is r̂ in test B?

\(\psi_0=5^\circ=0.087\) rad, \(\omega=2\pi\cdot1\ \text{Hz}=6.28\) rad/srmax = 0.55 rad/s
model \(b=1\) m, \(V=30\) m/s: \(\hat r_{\max}=0.55\cdot1/60\)= 0.009
spin \(\hat r\)0.1–0.4: 10 to 40 times larger

The engineer then writes

\[ C_n(\text{rotating})\approx\underbrace{C_n(\alpha,\beta)}_{\text{test A}}+\underbrace{C_{n_r}}_{\text{test B}}\,\hat r. \]

Inside the normal flight envelope this works: small \(\hat r\), attached flow, linear behaviour, and both tests are cheap and standard. The problem is not the method but carrying it outside the range where it holds.

Step 13

The central question: are these the same physical state?

One model oscillates: right, stop, left, stop. The other spins: same direction, never stopping, descending along a helix.

State A, forced oscillation, rate changing sign; state B, a spin, steady rotation. Does a number measured in A give the force in B?

No. Four concrete differences, all from steps 9–11:

  1. Size. \(\hat r\sim0.01\) in A, 0.1–0.4 in B. The slope near zero does not give the value far away (step 15).
  2. Sign. In A, \(r\) reverses every half cycle before an asymmetry can build. In B it keeps its sign: the right wing always slow and at high α, the left always fast and lower.
  3. Flow field. In A the separated region keeps breaking down and re-forming: a transient. In B it has settled: vortices, wakes and the shadow on the tail form a lasting structure matched to the rotation.
  4. Average. Over a cycle of A the mean is near zero and any lasting asymmetric moment is erased. In B the asymmetry is permanent, and it is exactly what is measured.

Knowing that they differ does not tell how much. The only way to know the force is to hold the model in that state, at high α and finite, constant \(\hat r\), and measure. That is where the rotary balance comes from.

Worked number: the tip angles over time

A: the right-tip α swings between \(40^\circ\pm1^\circ\) once a second, the left tip in antiphase; mean difference 0°.
B: right tip steadily 48°, left tip steadily 32° (step 11); the 16° difference never goes to zero.

Step 14

What the rotary balance does

Inside the tunnel. The model is on an arm that a motor turns at constant Ω about the flow axis, with the CG on that axis. At α = 45° the rotating model performs a spin motion. Try the α and r̂ sliders.

Definition

Rotary balance: a rig that turns the model steadily about an axis parallel to the free stream while an internal balance measures all six force and moment components. The model is set at the wanted α and β on the rotation axis.

Why the axis is parallel to the flow. In a real spin the aircraft turns about a vertical axis and falls along it, so relative to the aircraft the air comes along the rotation axis. In the tunnel the rotation axis is aligned with the tunnel flow: for the model the two are the same.

Projecting \(\vec\Omega\) (along the flow) onto body axes, with the x axis at α to the flow:

\[ p=\Omega\cos\alpha,\qquad r=\Omega\sin\alpha,\qquad q=0\ (\beta=0). \]

This is exactly the \((p,r)\) pair of step 11, produced from one Ω in an α-dependent ratio: mostly roll at low α, pure yaw at 90°.

Units check

\(\hat\Omega=\Omega b/2V\)dimensionless, like r̂
\(\hat p=\hat\Omega\cos\alpha,\ \hat r=\hat\Omega\sin\alpha\)dimensionless
\(\hat p^2+\hat r^2=\hat\Omega^2\)consistency check
\(\Omega\)rotation rate of the rig (and of the spin), parallel to the flowrad/s

stingrod holding the model from behind, on the arm and motor—
balancesix-component force gauge inside the model—

Worked number: one measurement point

\(\alpha=45^\circ\), \(\beta=0\), \(\hat\Omega=0.25\): \(\hat p=0.25\cos45^\circ=0.177\), \(\hat r=0.177\). While the arm turns, the rig reads \(C_n, C_l, C_m, C_L, C_D, C_Y\) directly. The result might be \(C_n=+0.012\): positive, with the rotation (step 16).

The engineer no longer estimates \(C_n\approx C_{n,\text{static}}+C_{n_r}\hat r\); the rig measures

\[ C_n=f(\alpha,\beta,\hat\Omega)\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad C_n=f(\alpha,\beta,\hat p,\hat r). \]

This is not the free-spin test, where a model is released and filmed: that shows the outcome (does it spin, how many turns) but measures no forces. The rotary balance imposes the motion and measures the forces. The two complement each other.

Step 15

Think geometrically: tangent or curve?

The most important picture of the lesson. Dashed line: the classical derivative, the slope at zero. Ochre curve: \(C_n(\hat r)\) as the rotary balance measures it. Shaded: the error between them. The 3D scene follows the same α and r̂.

Test B measures the slope near zero:

\[ C_{n_r}=\left.\frac{\partial C_n}{\partial\hat r}\right|_{\hat r\approx0} \]

A tangent represents a curve well near its point, and need not anywhere else. In Taylor form,

\[ C_n(\hat r)=C_n(0)+\left.\frac{dC_n}{d\hat r}\right|_0\hat r+\frac12\left.\frac{d^2C_n}{d\hat r^2}\right|_0\hat r^2+\frac16\left.\frac{d^3C_n}{d\hat r^3}\right|_0\hat r^3+\cdots \]

The classical model keeps the first two terms. The rest grow with \(\hat r^2\) and above: at \(\hat r=0.01\), \(\hat r^2=10^{-4}\); at \(\hat r=0.3\), \(\hat r^2=0.09\) and \(\hat r^3=0.027\), no longer negligible because the curvature is large at high α.

Units check

every term: \([C_n]=1,\ [\hat r^k]=1\)derivatives dimensionless too
linear model valid ⇔ \(|\tfrac12C_n''\hat r^2|\ll|C_{n_r}\hat r|\)i.e. \(\hat r\ll2|C_{n_r}|/|C_n''|\)

Worked number: read the chart

At \(\alpha=45^\circ\) the tangent slope is \(C_{n_r}=-0.06\). At \(\hat r=0.2\) the tangent predicts \(-0.06\cdot0.2=-0.012\), damping. The curve gives \(0.2\,(-0.06+1.2\cdot0.2-3.0\cdot0.04)=+0.012\). Same size, opposite sign: the classical model says brake where the real flow says throttle.

Set \(\alpha=5^\circ\) on the same chart: the curve sits on the line, and the linear model is right there.

A more precise forced-oscillation test does not fix this. The problem is not measurement accuracy but the quantity measured: however well the slope at zero is known, it does not give the curve at 0.3.

Step 16

Autorotation: the moment feeds the rotation

Ochre arc: the rotation. Red arc: the aerodynamic moment, in the same direction, the reverse of step 5. The right wing is fully separated, the left partly attached.

Definition

Autorotation: a rotation that produces an aerodynamic moment in its own direction and so sustains itself with no outside source.

Normal, stable aerodynamics: \(r>0\) gives \(C_n<0\). At high α the rotary balance measures, in some ranges, \(r>0\) with \(C_n>0\). The mechanism, from steps 9–11:

  1. Turning right, the right wing is slow and at high local α, the left fast and at lower α.
  2. At high α the right wing is more deeply separated: less lift, more drag (step 7).
  3. The extra drag on the right wing acts with an arm \(b/2\), pulls that wing back and turns the nose right: \(N>0\), with the rotation.
  4. The extra lift on the left wing rolls the aircraft right (step 10), raising the right wing’s local α further; back to 2.

Forcing the measured curve into step 6’s form defines an effective derivative \(C_{n_r,\text{eff}}=C_n/\hat r\). If it turns positive at high α:

\[ r>0\ \Rightarrow\ C_n>0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{rotation}\rightarrow\text{moment}\rightarrow\text{more rotation}. \]

Why a spin does not accelerate for ever

In the step 15 curve at 45°, \(C_n\) turns positive near \(\hat r\approx0.06\) and negative again near \(\hat r\approx0.34\). With \(I_z\,dr/dt=\bar q S b\,C_n(\hat r)\) the rotation speeds up between 0.06 and 0.34 and slows above 0.34. Where the curve crosses zero with negative slope, \(\hat r=0.34\), is a stable equilibrium spin: a state the aircraft settles into, not an event. (Inertia moments also take part in the real balance; this lesson shows the aerodynamic side only.)

Units check: the rotation equation

\(N=\bar q\,S\,b\,C_n\)[N/m²][m²][m] = [N·m]
\(dr/dt=N/I_z\)[N·m]/[kg·m²] = [rad/s²]

Worked number

\(\hat r=0.2\), \(\alpha=45^\circ\): \(C_n=+0.012\), \(N=0.012\cdot882\,000=+10\,600\) N·m. With \(I_z=60\,000\) kg·m², \(dr/dt=0.18\) rad/s²: the rotation gains 0.18 rad/s every second until \(\hat r=0.34\) (\(r=4.1\) rad/s, 1.5 s per turn).

\(C_{n_r}\) is not a fixed property of the aircraft: it is only the slope near \(\hat r\approx0\), and even that depends on α. At finite \(\hat r\) the derivative gives way to the whole curve, \(C_n=f(\alpha,\beta,\hat\Omega)\).

Step 17

An important correction: “flow memory”

On the oscillating model the grey arrow is the tail motion and the red arrow the force, which arrives late. On the steadily turning model the flow has settled and the asymmetry is fixed.

A common sentence: “forced oscillation cannot see flow memory, the rotary balance can.” That is too strong and partly wrong.

Definitions

Flow memory (unsteady aerodynamics): the loads depend on the recent past as well as the present α, β and rates. Separated regions take time to form and decay, so the force follows a change in angle with a delay.

Phase lag \(\varphi\): the angle between a sinusoidal motion and its response. Zero lag is an instantaneous force; towards 90° the force depends on the rate rather than the position.

Test B’s output is the in-phase and the 90°-shifted part of the response, and the shifted part is precisely a measure of lag; that is why \(C_{n_{\dot\beta}}\) exists. Testing at several frequencies gives the frequency dependence of the lag. Forced oscillation does say something real about time-dependent aerodynamics. The accurate distinction is

\[ \text{forced oscillation}\rightarrow\text{time-dependent response to small or moderate oscillation} \] \[ \text{rotary balance}\rightarrow\text{settled aerodynamics of steady rotation at a finite rate} \]
Static\(C_n(\alpha,\beta)\); no rotationtest A
Forced oscillationslope at \(\hat r\approx0\) and phase lag; combinations such as \(C_{n_r}-C_{n_{\dot\beta}}\cos\alpha\)test B
Rotary balance\(C_n(\alpha,\beta,\hat\Omega)\); the full curve at steady, finite rotationthis lesson
Large-amplitude, oscillatory coninghysteresis, spin entry and recovery, the memory itselffurther tests

Strength and limit of the rotary balance

Strength: it measures directly the nonlinear coefficients of steady rotation. Limit: because it is steady, it does not by itself give the moments when the rate is changing: spin entry, recovery, hysteresis. Those need time-dependent models and other unsteady tests. The rotary balance maps the equilibrium spin and the autorotation region; it is not a film of the transitions.

The two tests answer different questions. Oscillation: how late does the force follow the motion? Rotary balance: what is the force in steady rotation? A full spin model uses both.

Derivation, step 18

How \(p\) and \(r\) change the local α along the span

The formula previewed in step 11, built in full. The ochre curve above the wing is \(\alpha_{\text{local}}-\alpha\) along the span; the tip arrows show where the air comes from.

1. Velocity of a point on a rigid body

\[ \vec V_{\text{pt}}=\vec V_{\text{CG}}+\vec\omega\times\vec r_{\text{pt}} \]

In body axes \(\vec V_{\text{CG}}=(u_0,v_0,w_0)\) with \(u_0=V\cos\alpha\cos\beta\), \(v_0=V\sin\beta\), \(w_0=V\sin\alpha\cos\beta\). With \(\beta=0\): \(\vec V_{\text{CG}}=(V\cos\alpha,\,0,\,V\sin\alpha)\).

\(u,v,w\)body components of velocity: forward, right, downm/s
\(\alpha=\arctan(w/u)\)angle of attack from the components, consistent with step 1rad
\(\eta=y/(b/2)\)span position: −1 left tip, 0 root, +1 right tip—

2. The cross product

\(\vec\omega=(p,q,r)\), \(\vec r_{\text{pt}}=(0,y,0)\):

\[ \vec\omega\times\vec r_{\text{pt}}=(q\cdot0-r\,y,\ r\cdot0-p\cdot0,\ p\,y-q\cdot0)=(-r\,y,\ 0,\ p\,y) \]

\(q\) drops out: pitching does not change the velocity of points level with the CG (zero arm). That is why \(p\) and \(r\) matter for a spin and \(q\) is secondary.

3. Local velocity and local α

\[ u(y)=V\cos\alpha-r\,y,\qquad w(y)=V\sin\alpha+p\,y \] \[ \alpha_{\text{local}}(y)=\arctan\frac{V\sin\alpha+p\,y}{V\cos\alpha-r\,y} \]

Dividing by \(V\), with \(p\,y/V=\hat p\,\eta\) and \(r\,y/V=\hat r\,\eta\):

\[ \alpha_{\text{local}}(\eta)=\arctan\frac{\sin\alpha+\hat p\,\eta}{\cos\alpha-\hat r\,\eta} \]

Units check

\(p\,y,\ r\,y\)[rad/s][m] = [m/s]
\(p\,y/V=(pb/2V)\cdot y/(b/2)=\hat p\,\eta\)1·1 = 1
arctan argument[m/s]/[m/s] = 1

4. Linear form for small rotation

For \(\hat p\eta,\hat r\eta\ll1\), to first order:

\[ \alpha_{\text{local}}\approx\alpha+\eta\,(\hat p\cos\alpha+\hat r\sin\alpha) \]

\(\Delta\alpha\) is linear along the span and weights \(\hat p\) by \(\cos\alpha\) and \(\hat r\) by \(\sin\alpha\): \(\hat p\) dominates at low α, \(\hat r\) contributes as much at high α. With the rotary-balance relations \(p=\Omega\cos\alpha\), \(r=\Omega\sin\alpha\) this becomes \(\Delta\alpha=\eta\,\hat\Omega\): in a spin the local-α difference scales directly with \(\hat\Omega\).

Worked number

\(V=60\), \(\alpha=30^\circ\), \(\hat p=0.15\), \(\hat r=0.10\): \(p=0.15\cdot120/10=1.8\) rad/s, \(r=1.2\) rad/s. At the tip \(y=5\) m: \(p\,y=9\) m/s, \(r\,y=6\) m/s.

Right tip: \(u=60\cdot0.866-6=45.96\), \(w=60\cdot0.5+9=39.0\), \(\alpha=\arctan(39.0/45.96)=40.3^\circ\).
Left tip: \(u=51.96+6=57.96\), \(w=30-9=21.0\), \(\alpha=19.9^\circ\).

Linear form: \(\Delta\alpha=\pm(0.15\cdot0.866+0.10\cdot0.5)=\pm0.18\) rad \(=\pm10.3^\circ\), giving 40.3° and 19.7°, almost exact at these rates. The left tip is below the stall peak and the right tip above it: one aircraft, one instant, two regimes.

The difference itself may be linear; the trouble is its effect. \(C_L(\alpha)\) has opposite slopes on either side of the peak, so a 10° difference near the peak reverses the lift difference. Step 19 shows exactly that.

Derivation, step 19

\(C_l\)–\(\hat p\) and \(C_n\)–\(\hat r\): damping, zero, autorotation

The aircraft rolls right at p̂. At small α the descending right wing lifts more and brakes the roll (green arc). Take α past the stall peak: the same wing lifts less and the arc turns red.

1. Strip theory for \(C_{l_p}\)

Cut the wing into thin strips, each seeing its own local α. With roll only, \(\Delta\alpha(y)=p\,y/V\) (small α, \(\cos\alpha\approx1\)). The extra lift of a strip, with lift slope \(C_{L\alpha}\):

\[ dL=\bar q\,c(y)\,C_{L\alpha}\,\frac{p\,y}{V}\,dy \]

It pushes the descending wing up, against the roll, with arm \(y\):

\[ L_{\text{roll}}=-\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}y\,dL=-\bar q\,C_{L\alpha}\frac{p}{V}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}c(y)\,y^2\,dy \]

For a rectangular wing (\(c\) constant, \(S=cb\)), \(\int y^2dy=b^3/12\):

\[ C_l=\frac{L_{\text{roll}}}{\bar qSb}=-C_{L\alpha}\frac{p}{V}\frac{c\,b^3/12}{c\,b\cdot b}=-C_{L\alpha}\frac{pb}{2V}\cdot\frac16\ \Longrightarrow\ C_{l_p}=-\frac{C_{L\alpha}}{6} \]

Units check

\(dL=\bar q\,c\,C_{L\alpha}(py/V)\,dy\)[N/m²][m][1/rad][rad][m] = [N]
\(y\,dL\)[N·m]
\(C_{l_p}=-C_{L\alpha}/6\)[1/rad]

The sign is the whole story. Left of the peak \(C_{L\alpha}>0\) and \(C_{l_p}<0\): roll damping, what keeps an aircraft level by itself. At the peak \(C_{L\alpha}=0\) and the damping vanishes. Right of the peak \(C_{L\alpha}<0\) and \(C_{l_p}>0\): autorotation. Same physics, same wing, same formula, opposite sign.

2. The yaw side: drag asymmetry

The descending, higher-α wing is deeper in stall and makes more drag. Extra drag on the right wing turns the nose right: \(\Delta C_n\propto+\Delta C_D\cdot\eta\). Rolling right causes yawing right, and yawing right (steps 9–11) raises the right wing’s α: the loop closes. This is the physical source of \(C_{n_r,\text{eff}}\) turning positive at high α.

Worked number

From the curve: at \(\alpha=10^\circ\), \(C_{L\alpha}\approx0.09\)/° \(=5.2\)/rad, so \(C_{l_p}=-0.86\), a strong brake. At \(19^\circ\), \(C_{L\alpha}\approx0\) and \(C_{l_p}\approx0\). At \(23^\circ\), \(C_{L\alpha}\approx-0.05\)/° \(=-2.9\)/rad, so \(C_{l_p}=+0.48\): the roll feeds itself. Just past the peak is the most dangerous region.

Autorotation does not need 60° or more: it starts as soon as \(C_{L\alpha}\) turns negative, around 20–25°. At very high α the curve flattens again and autorotation can weaken, as the chart shows.

Step 20

One picture

High α, steady rotation, asymmetric separation, a helical path: every step of the lesson in one scene.

The dynamic derivative gives the slope near \(\hat r\approx0\); the rotary balance measures the actual force at finite \(\hat r\).

Steps 0–2 · language and symmetry
Body axes (x forward, y right, z down) and \(p,q,r\); α between chord and relative wind; with no rotation both wings see the same air.
Steps 3–5 · coefficients and damping
\(C_n=N/(\bar qSb)\); \(\hat r=rb/2V\), tip rotational speed over flight speed; \(C_{n_r}=\partial C_n/\partial\hat r<0\) is the tail braking the rotation, \(\tau=2VI_z/(\bar qSb^2|C_{n_r}|)\).
Step 6 · linear model
\(C_n=C_{n_\beta}\beta+C_{n_r}\hat r+C_{n_{\delta_r}}\delta_r+\dots\), first-order Taylor; needs small disturbances and attached flow.
Step 7 · high α
Separation, vortex breakdown, tail blanketing; past the peak the lift slope is negative and effects no longer add.
Steps 8–11 · steady rotation
\(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) has opposite signs at the tips: \(V\mp r\,y\); lift difference through \(L\propto V^2\); \(\alpha_{\text{local}}=\arctan\frac{V\sin\alpha+py}{V\cos\alpha-ry}\); two wings, two regimes.
Steps 12–13 · classical method and its limit
Static + forced oscillation at \(\hat r\approx0.01\); a spin is \(\hat r\approx0.1\)–0.4, steady, with settled flow: not the same physical state.
Step 14 · rotary balance
Model turned at constant Ω about an axis parallel to the flow; \(p=\Omega\cos\alpha\), \(r=\Omega\sin\alpha\); \(C_n=f(\alpha,\beta,\hat\Omega)\) measured directly.
Steps 15–16 · tangent versus curve, autorotation
The derivative is the tangent at zero; at finite \(\hat r\) the curve departs and changes sign; with \(C_n>0\) the rotation feeds itself, and an equilibrium spin sits where the curve crosses zero with negative slope.
Step 17 · correction
Forced oscillation does see phase lag; the distinction is oscillatory response versus steady-rotation aerodynamics. Hysteresis needs other tests.
Steps 18–19 · derivations
\(\Delta\alpha=\eta(\hat p\cos\alpha+\hat r\sin\alpha)\); \(C_{l_p}=-C_{L\alpha}/6\) reverses sign past the peak; drag asymmetry feeds the yaw.

What this settles: why classical dynamic derivatives such as \(C_{l_p}\) and \(C_{n_r}\) may not be enough to predict a spin. They are the initial slope of a curve in the linear world; the spin lives far along that curve, where it has bent and changed sign. The only way to see it there is to go there: turn the model and measure.
